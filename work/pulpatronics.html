Pulpatronics

Rebuilding the visual system for a paper-RFID startup, then producing everything that had to live inside it.

NFC UI/UX Brand
Problem

A startup selling an unproven material into an industry that buys on reliability. The old palette was earthy and quiet, and the product needed to read as new.

What I did

The only designer in a team of eight. I rebuilt the colour system, then produced everything that had to live inside it: promotional material and the visual support for their workshops.

Result

A visual system the team could keep using without me, and a brand that reads as new and dependable at the same time.

🔒 This page shows the part of my PulpaTronics work I’m free to share.

Project overview

PulpaTronics makes RFID tags out of paper. No metal antenna, no chip, no laminate, which is the whole point: over 18 billion RFID tags are manufactured for single use every year, and a million items carrying a PulpaTronics tag instead saves around 2,400kg of CO2 a year. It's a B2B sustainability play, and it was pitching to retail brands that judge a supplier partly on whether it looks like it will still exist in three years.

I joined as a design intern in London in 2024, into a team of eight, as the only designer in the company. The brief was brand building, which at that size meant whatever the brand needed that week, decided by me.

My contributions

Three strands, and each one grew out of the one before it.

  • Rebuilding the product's visual system, starting with colour. I audited the existing palette, found what was making it read as tired, and remapped every token in the system to a replacement. That set the constraints everything after it was made inside.
  • Design material support. Once the system existed, I produced against it: marketing collateral, business cards, an illustration set for a joint campaign, and visual support for customer workshops.
  • UI/UX design, for PulpaTronics and for the ventures it worked alongside. Interface and experience work for the company's own product surfaces and for the partner startups it ran joint work with, including the website design standards. Most of this strand is under NDA, and it was a large share of what I made there.

Rebuilding the visual system

The old palette was three colours: a deep Business Blue, a Paper White, and a Peace Blue turquoise that existed in the spec but was barely used anywhere. In practice the brand ran on desaturated earthy tones, dark blues, browns and greys, with beige and off-white doing most of the work. It read as faded paper, and PulpaTronics was a technology company asking retailers to bet on it.

What the brand actually had to do

The positioning was the real constraint. PulpaTronics is a B2B startup selling an unproven material into an industry that buys on reliability, so the brand had to feel dependable and new at once: steady enough for a supplier, and fresh enough to be worth choosing over the incumbent.

The old palette leaned towards established: earthy, low-contrast and quiet. I wanted it to read as early and inevitable, a company on the move. In practice that meant keeping the deep navy, because trust is what a supplier sells, and finally putting the turquoise to work to show momentum.

What I changed

  • Put the highlight colour to work. The turquoise was already in the brand book. I gave it a job: a small, deliberate accent that gives every layout a focal point.
  • Redefined the neutrals. Cleaner beige and off-white, so the paper reference reads as intentional.
  • Balanced reliable against innovative. The deep navy stays, because trustworthiness is what a B2B supplier is selling. Brighter mid-tones carry the rest.
  • Aligned the palette with the mission. A blue-green range reinforces the environmental argument without anyone having to say the word "eco".

The mapping is where the work actually is. A muddy warm black came out of the system entirely. The navy was cleaned and deepened. The mid blue lifted substantially, from a near-grey rgba(40,54,65) to rgba(69,114,134). A neutral grey became an actual light blue. Pure white softened to paper white, so nothing on the page glares.

The most important thing I learned

Being the decision maker

PulpaTronics was eight people, and I was the only designer in it. At that size I wrote my own brief and looked after the brand myself.

In a large company you take a problem as far as your level allows, lay out two or three routes, and hand them up for someone senior to choose. At eight people, I knew the palette better than anyone, so I researched it, taught myself what I was missing, and decided.

The job became owning the outcome: research it, learn what I don’t know, choose, and bring the finished thing.

Bringing it to people mattered most. The role asks you to get people who aren’t designers, and who are busy with something else, to trust a judgement they can’t check themselves. I earned that by arriving with a resolved answer and the reasoning behind it, and it made me the owner of the work.

It also means the brand is never one company's problem for long. A hardware startup selling into retail lives in other people's contexts: joint campaigns, partner brands, other London startups whose own identity sits next to yours. The illustration set was for a campaign run with Petit Pli, and a chunk of my remaining time went to collaboration pages and UI support where PulpaTronics appeared as somebody else’s partner.

What that actually trained

  • Validating my own decisions. At this size I signed off my own palette, so I showed for myself that the old one was costing the company something, then said so in language a founder and an engineer both found reasonable.
  • Letting the system carry the brand. One designer can’t be in every deck, email and trade-show banner, so testing the wordmark on every background in the palette meant other people made good choices on days I wasn’t in the room.
  • Designing at the speed the company moves. Eighteen illustrations existed because a campaign had a date. Line work kept them quick to finish and still good.
  • Working with people who don't speak design. Customer workshops, engineers, commercial leads, partner brands. Most of my day was spent with non-designers, and that turned out to be the most useful part.

This is what I took from a startup and kept. Everything I've built since, including founding my own venture, runs on it.

Alongside the design work, the job is being the person who decides, and who can say why clearly enough that other people back the decision.
