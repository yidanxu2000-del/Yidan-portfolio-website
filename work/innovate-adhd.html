Innovate ADHD

An ADHD assessment that people with ADHD can finish.

Designed for people with ADHD and their families. The 0 to 1 for an AI ADHD company, live in two weeks.

This page runs on the product’s rules. One idea at a time, large type, high contrast. If it reads easily, that is the case study.

For
People with ADHD, and their families.
The goal
Get them into our service faster, and lay the ground for the AI features to come.
My role
Product manager, the only designer, and the person doing the marketing.
Timeline
From nothing to live in two weeks, 2025.

The problem

Somebody who suspects they have ADHD has three things to get through first, each built for a different kind of reader.

01 · Clinic sites are too complex to find what you came for

Small type, long navigation, paragraphs before anything happens. Counted off the first one:

19links in the first screen.
13navigation items.
150words, about, before you scroll.

A lot of attention to spend before you have learned anything.

02 · The self-test is a long questionnaire, and ADHD makes it hard to finish

Eighteen questions, five lines of text each, and at the end you add up your own score across two parts.

The form asks for the exact executive function ADHD takes away. People started it, stopped partway, and never reached care.

03 · The two are always apart, so people drop out between them

You read the site, then you register somewhere else, then a questionnaire arrives by email, and you have to remember to go back and finish it.

Every step is somewhere to drop out, and this is the audience least able to carry a plan across them. None of the three pages above keeps the test on the page.

What I did

The rule I set myself: spend as little of a person’s attention as possible before they know whether they need help.

01 · A site designed for an ADHD reader

The site had been built for a neurotypical reader, like the clinic pages.

So I wrote new rules: one highlight colour so the eye lands where it should, larger type, more space, one clear action per screen.

Heading 1 · Onest 48 Semibold

Transform ADHD

Heading 2 · Onest 34 Semibold

Quick Assessment

Heading 3 · Onest 24 Semibold

What the result means

Body · Onest 18 Regular

Struggling with focus, overwhelm, or feeling misunderstood?

Link · Onest 18 Regular · #8E5D04

Book an assessment

Ratios are the text colour on each ground. The highlight is a fill at 1.8:1 as text, so links take a darker tone, #8E5D04.

02 · The questionnaire, gamified, inside the site

We were the first to put the screener inside the page itself. You answer without an account or a second visit, and the result appears right there.

Each question arrives on its own, answered by dragging a slider, with a face that reacts as you move it.

Underneath is the ASRS’s own screener, Part A: six questions out of the eighteen, scored the way a clinic scores them.

Four or more and it tells you to go and get assessed. Shorter, still clinically valid.

Before

After

Try it yourself

03 · From the result straight into our service

When the test ends, the result screen points straight at our service. One tap goes into booking, with named clinicians in the same flow, so nobody has to find their way back to it later.

What they said

Four people with ADHD used it, and I sat with them.

“It felt easier. Other sites have so much text that I have no idea where to look.”

“The interaction is actually interesting. The old ones are so dull. I finished it, and I got my result quickly. I didn’t have to register, wait for a screening questionnaire, then remember to answer an email. That kills any motivation to do the test. Here I could see my result and what to do next, right on the page.”

“This would work for the full twenty-four-question version. I think far fewer people would give up halfway.”

“Cute site. I like it.”

“The logic is really clear, and the thing that made me happy is that I actually finished the basic ADHD test. I like it. It’s genuinely fun.”

A teammate who has ADHD, testing the live assessment. Anonymised at their request.

Each said the same thing: easy to move through, and they knew quickly whether they needed help.

How I think about this

I design for ADHD.

Executive function is the expensive part.

So I build the thing to cost as little concentration as possible.

This page is built the same way.

How two weeks was possible

AI sat inside a deliberate chain, and I stayed on the code myself at the end.

01
Figma prototypes
Fast, low-fidelity concepts shared early, so developers were building the right thing from day one.
02
AI-driven build
Vibe coding alongside the prototypes, turning ideas into working features fast.
03
AI-enabled validation
A second pass on usability and interaction clarity, before it reached real users.
04
Claude-assisted implementation
Refining and modifying developer-written code myself, so the build matched the intended experience.

What else I owned

  • Roadmap and delivery. What we built and in what order, live in two weeks.
  • Requirements. The literature and people with ADHD, turned into a spec, with the leanest assessment model on the market as the base.
  • Priorities. The assessment first. Booking, content pages and the design system after.
  • Clinicians and developers. Clinicians signed off that it stayed valid, developers shipped it, and I made the trade-offs explicit.
  • Numbers. I picked what to measure before we shipped.
  • Positioning and early growth. How the product was described and where it went out.
