I use AI to explore wide, and I choose what stays.

This page covers the loop I run with AI, and what building this way has taught me about how much a system should decide for people.

Part one

How I work with AI.

The rule underneath everything

AI gives me more options, and I decide what to keep

Ask AI for options and it gives you plenty: features, sections, copy, variants. That’s useful, and it puts the real work in the choosing. A product gets good when someone with taste keeps the parts that earn their place.

So AI helps me explore, and I do the editing. Deciding what to cut is a judgement about people, whether it’s a product decision or a single screen.

Everything below is the same idea, applied stage by stage.

I use several models and let them check each other

For anything that matters, I give the same problem to more than one model and compare the answers.

Claude does the reasoning and the writing, Replit and Claude Code do the building, and Figma Make and Claude help with visuals. Where their answers differ is where I look hardest, because that’s usually where the real question is.

Markdown is what makes this work. I keep the context of a project in .md files: the research findings, the decisions already made, the constraints, the tone. That file travels between tools. It means I can move a problem from one model to another without re-explaining it, and it means the reasoning is written down somewhere a person can read too. The file outlives any one tool.

Why it matters

Getting good at one model took a few weeks. Learning where each one slips, and building a way to catch it, came from real projects.

The five stages, and who does what

This is the loop I run. The closer the work is to a person, the more of it I do by hand.

  1. 01

    Talking to people. Mine.

    Interviews, workshops, sitting with someone while they use the thing. I run these myself and write them up in Notion, because the pauses and hesitations are where I learn the most.

    Where AI comes in: once the raw material exists, I use AI to cluster transcripts by problem type and pull the core points back out. It saves days of manual tagging, and I still decide what matters.

  2. 02

    Turning findings into the real question

    Here I ask AI to play the critic: what’s wrong with this product as it stands, what would a sceptical user say, and where does the argument break. Then I take those problems into the design myself.

    Starting from the objections gives me a sharper brief.

  3. 03

    Designing, fast, and then cutting

    Figma Make and Claude generate, and I explore widely, since ten directions now take the time two used to. When something needs real craft I connect Figma over MCP and refine it there, with AI helping on the detail.

    Then I edit. Choosing what ships is the real work.

  4. 04

    Testing with AI first, then with people

    Before I put anything in front of a person I have AI walk the flow and tell me where it loses the thread. It catches the obvious breakage cheaply.

    Then real users, every time. I A/B test the decisions that matter and check whether the workflow holds, whether it’s clear, and whether people can finish. People are the ones who tell me whether it feels right.

  5. 05

    Shipping, and then still being there

    Claude Code writes it, GitHub holds it, and it deploys straight from the repository. This site works that way and goes live within a minute of a merge. After launch I keep looking after what I’ve shipped.

I was once the second person

Imagine I hand in my notice and someone new opens the folder. I want them to find a system and a record they can carry on from.

I think about this because I’ve been that second person. I was handed a set of polished interfaces, mostly made with AI, with no record of the research or the thinking behind each section, and asked to improve them. That’s when I learned how much the record matters.

So every project now leaves a design and memory file behind. Tokens, components, the decisions and the reasons behind them, all written down.

Leaving a system behind has always been part of the work, like versioning a release, and AI work needs that memory file too.

What it looked like on 1Soul

Founding product manager, one designer, an AI matchmaking product. The whole loop ran on it.

  • By hand: four in-depth interviews with London university students, plus eight online interviews with heavy users of social and dating apps. Written up in Notion.
  • AI extracted: the transcripts clustered by problem type. Eight areas came out, with brand tone and trust for women at the top.
  • AI attacked: I had it read the site as a sceptical first-time visitor. It found the same thing the interviews had, which is how I knew the finding was real.
  • Human judgement: the mechanic. She gets a clue first, and the decision stays hers. That came from sitting with one participant's discomfort, and no model would have proposed it.
  • AI built: positioning into copy, copy into Figma, Figma into shipped code. Research finding to live redesign in about twenty hours.
  • Measured: female sign-ups up 25%.

AI sped up everything after each decision, and the decisions stayed with people.

The short version

Five things I hold to.

  • Anything involving a person, I do myself. Interviews, workshops, watching someone use the product. That’s where the real findings come from.
  • Use more than one model. Where two answers differ is where the real question sits.
  • Keep the context in markdown. It moves between tools, it survives the tool going away, and a person can read it.
  • AI generates, I edit. Choosing what ships is where the value is.
  • Leave a memory file. I’ve been the second person on a project, and I know how much a written record helps.
Part two

What building this way taught me.

The method above is how the work gets made. This part is what kept turning up while making it: the same question in two very different products, and what I now believe about it.

Starting point

AI becomes useful once it has the right job

Every project that touches AI teaches me the same thing. It becomes useful once it has a specific job inside real work, so I shape the AI around that work.

So I give AI one clear job at a time, like clustering interview transcripts by problem type, narrowing a field of candidates before a person decides, or holding the context of a conversation so a driver doesn’t have to. Each one takes real work off someone, and the judgement about what matters stays with me or with the user.

Two projects show this most clearly. In 1Soul, AI sits between a person and a decision about a relationship. In the Xiaomi Auto cockpit, software sits between a person and a car moving at speed. They turned out to share one design problem.

The common thread: show people where the line is

Both projects put a system between a person and a moment that matters: who they’ll meet, or what their car does next.

In both, trust breaks when the system decides more than the person realised. So I draw a clear line between what the system narrows down and what the person decides, and the product says it in the same words every time.

1Soul: AI narrows the field, she decides

Research turned up one specific fear: a match arrives with a name and nothing else, and you’re asked to trust an algorithm about who to meet. I answered it with a mechanic. Cupid narrows the field, then gives her one small human clue, and going further is her call. The product says so every time, so the AI stays at “worth considering” and she decides who’s worth meeting.

Xiaomi Auto: the cockpit narrates itself

A cockpit draws the same line with more at stake. The system is always choosing what to show, from a navigation update to a driving-assist prompt or an alert on the rear display, while the driver stays in control at speed.

So the work was about order and weight: what the interface says right now, and what it saves for later. The driver’s attention stays on the road, and every suggestion reads as a suggestion, with the choice left open.

Put side by side, they gave me a rule I now use everywhere. An interface earns the right to automate more by first making it completely clear what it is leaving to you.

A closer look: diagnosing why a dating product didn't feel trustworthy

When I audited 1Soul’s homepage, my question was whether a stranger would believe this is a serious product built for them.

That splits into four things a visitor checks in the first few seconds, roughly in this order. Is this real, or a novelty? Who is actually using it? Does it understand why I hate the alternatives? Would meeting someone through it be safe? The homepage had a gap at all four.

The plainest example was the copy. The headline was “Your agent is already dating on your behalf.” It was meant to sound futuristic, and it touched exactly what a first-time visitor fears: that the AI is the one making contact. It’s the same boundary problem as above, this time inside one sentence. I rewrote the hero around a plainer promise for people who want something serious, and moved “AI narrows this down for you” further down the page, where people already trust the product enough to want the shortcut.

The brand colour told the same story. The site ran on a deep maroon, and two testers separately called it “like a magic book”, moody and mysterious. That suits a fantasy app, and 1Soul is about your next serious relationship, so I moved to a palette that tells people straight away what kind of product they’re looking at.

Safety got its own section. Every interview came back to the same worry: meeting a stranger from an app. The homepage answered it with one reassuring sentence buried in the middle of the page.

So I pulled safety out into a section of its own, and rewrote it around things you can check. Verified student and real-identity accounts. First meetings at verified campus venues. Background context on a match before you meet. Visible ID checks at in-person events.

The rule I took from it, and still use: a safety claim works when you can point at the feature behind it.

Then I reordered the rest of the homepage to match the order a visitor needs answers in. Pain points first, in the words real users used: dating fatigue, conversations that go nowhere, feeling disrespected. Then every feature turned into the thing it protects, so "not endless swiping" becomes "every match is vetted before you see it". Social proof and the playful ideas come last, including a “Secret Match” campus event that we kept as a surprise.

Together, these closed the gap between what the founders knew about 1Soul and what a stranger could tell in five seconds.

A closer look: what eight months building system UI taught me about HMI

Shipping system UI across a product line means the interface has to hold up across car variants, screen sizes, hardware configurations and languages, Chinese first and then English once the international rollout started. The logic underneath has to work across all of them.

The clearest lesson was about design covering for hardware. Apple’s Dynamic Island turned a front-camera cutout into a feature, and at Xiaomi Auto that kind of save happens all the time, quietly.

Each device has its own comfortable reach zone for a driver’s hand. A rear-entertainment screen added to one variant could, in an edge case, partly block the rear-view mirror. China’s car industry moves fast, and design is often the layer that makes new hardware easy to use. My mentor once took three separate hardware hubs and, through interaction design, made them read as two.

I also learned to use AI as a second check on my own judgement. I run a shadow system or a colour decision back through AI to catch what my eye has got used to, and I keep my own craft sharp enough to do the same work by hand. AI sharpens decisions I’ve already made.

In practice I put Figma Make and Gemini inside the design loop, well beyond inspiration. I could explore many more directions in the time it used to take to sketch two or three, so more options got a proper look.

The more useful habit went the other way. I’d give AI a colour system I had already chosen, ask it to optimise for contrast and hierarchy on its own, and compare its answer to mine. That shows whether a choice holds up.

I did the same with grouping and layout. On a screen with many competing functions, I let AI find its own path through the information architecture, and anywhere it differed from my structure became a question to look into. AI pressure-tested the design, and the design stayed mine.

The last lesson was about scale. At a startup I owned projects end to end, from research to shipped product. At a company the size of Xiaomi, each person owns one link in a long chain, and doing it well means knowing your link well enough to hand off cleanly in both directions. I now see my range as being able to join that chain at almost any link and move it forward.

Working principles

What I keep coming back to.

  • AI narrows the field, and the person makes the choice. People trust a system that clearly decides with them.
  • Trust starts in the interface. A privacy promise lands when the interface itself is open about what it’s doing.
  • Say the boundary out loud. Repeating “You decide, always” in the product tells people who’s in control.
  • Good AI-assisted work feels considered. People using the shipped product notice the care, whichever parts AI helped speed up.
